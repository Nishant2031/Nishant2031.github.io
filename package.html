Warpage and solder-joint fatigue in a CoWoS-style AI package

The die from my cold plate project, mounted the way AI accelerators are: on a silicon interposer, soldered to an organic substrate. I modelled how the package bends and how the solder bumps fatigue under temperature cycling, turned that into lifetime statistics, and checked the mechanics against a finite-element model I wrote from scratch.

The problem

AI accelerators put the logic die and its memory side by side on a silicon interposer (TSMC calls this CoWoS), and solder the interposer to an organic substrate with C4 bumps. Silicon expands at about 2.6 ppm/K and the substrate at about 15 ppm/K. Every temperature swing therefore shears the bump layer and bends the whole package like a bimetal strip.

I modelled a 30 mm, 1 kW die on a 50 mm interposer, surrounded by mold compound, on a 70 mm substrate. The C4 bumps are SAC305 solder, 90 µm across at 150 µm pitch and 80 µm tall, with capillary underfill around them. The load is the standard JEDEC JESD22-A104 condition G cycle: −40 to 125 °C, 15-minute ramps and dwells.

Approach

  1. Two plates joined by a shear layer. Along the package diagonal, from the centre to the corner, the top plate is interposer plus die (or plus mold) and the bottom plate is the substrate. The C4 layer between them carries shear. Force balance and strain compatibility give two coupled equations for the bump shear strain γ(x) and the force N(x) it builds up. N bends both plates to a common curvature, and integrating that curvature gives the warpage. This is Suhir's bimaterial-assembly model, which I generalised to plates that change along the length and to a non-linear bond, and solved numerically.
  2. Viscoplastic solder. SAC305 creeps even at room temperature, so the bump stress follows the Anand model, with published SAC305 constants. It runs at every node, integrated implicitly so 10-second time steps stay stable. The underfill around the bumps is elastic below its glass transition.
  3. Damage per cycle. At the corner bump (the highest distance from the neutral point), I take the stress–strain loop of the third cycle. From it I get the creep strain-energy density ΔW and the accumulated creep strain, and convert those to cycles to failure with Syed's 2004 SnAgCu models. Engelmaier's strain-range model is run alongside for comparison.
  4. Statistics. 120 Monte Carlo runs vary bump height and diameter, substrate CTE and modulus, and underfill modulus. Each run's life is then multiplied by Weibull scatter (slope 6) to represent microstructure. From that population I simulate a 77-part test-to-failure and fit it the way a reliability engineer would.
  5. Field life. The same model runs on a data-centre power cycle, idle to full load. The junction temperatures come from my cold plate design. I then compare the result with the Norris–Landzberg acceleration factor that industry uses.

The two Syed models agree within 13%. Engelmaier predicts about nine times longer life. Its constants come from tin-lead joints on leadless parts, so I use it only as a sanity bound and quote the energy model.

A millimetre of warpage on a bare package is realistic. It is the reason production CoWoS packages carry a copper lid or stiffener ring, which this model does not include yet.

  • Substrate CTE is the strongest lever on both outputs. Going from 15 to 10 ppm/K raises bump life almost four times and cuts warpage by 43%. This is why low-CTE glass-cloth cores and glass substrates are being developed for AI packages.
  • A stiffer underfill extends life and leaves warpage almost unchanged. A stiffer bond keeps the shear zone shorter, so the corner bump strains less.
  • Interposer size does not change bump life, but it does change warpage. With underfill, the corner strain is set by how quickly the bond layer transfers load (a decay length of a few tenths of a millimetre), not by the distance to the neutral point. Moving from a 50 mm to a 70 mm interposer leaves bump life the same and raises warpage from 640 to 1,040 µm. For underfilled area arrays, the simple "strain grows with DNP" rule is the wrong mental model.
  • Taller bumps last longer. The same mismatch spreads over more solder height.

From one number to a lifetime distribution

One deterministic life is not what a qualification engineer reports. Manufacturing scatter alone spreads the model's median life from 2,040 to 3,530 cycles (11% coefficient of variation). Substrate CTE dominates (rank correlation −0.75), followed by bump height (+0.38) and underfill modulus (+0.32).

Field life. In a data centre, the chip cycles between idle (about 38 °C) and full load (69 °C, the junction temperature of my cold plate design). I assumed 4 load cycles a day. Running my model on that cycle gives an acceleration factor of 152 over the chamber test. The industry Norris–Landzberg equation with SAC constants gives 162, a 6% difference from two unrelated routes. At that factor, B1 is more than a century of service. Even at 24 cycles a day, it is about 29 years.

For this design, C4 thermal fatigue in the field is not what limits life. The real risks sit earlier: assembly at 217 °C, where the package carries its largest strain and warpage, and the accelerated test itself.

Checking the mechanics independently

The 1D model makes strong assumptions: thin-plate bending, a bond layer that carries only shear, and no free-edge effects. To test them, I wrote a separate axisymmetric finite-element code with 8-node quadrilaterals, 2,431 elements and 15,000 unknowns. It solves the same stack in full 2D elasticity, with the bond layer as a real solid. Both models were cooled elastically from 125 °C to −40 °C.

The number that drives fatigue, the shear strain at the corner bump, agrees within 2%. Halving the FE mesh size moved it by 0.8%. The 1D model's peak strain is 21% too high, but that peak is at the bare edge, where no bump sits.

Warpage over the substrate rim is the weakest result, at 15% high. In the 1D model, the bare rim is a straight extension. In the disc model it is a ring that also has to bend around the circumference, which stiffens it.

The time-dependent solution is converged too. Cutting the time step from 20 s to 5 s changes life by 0.3%, running four cycles instead of two changes it by 1.4%, and nearly tripling the grid density changes it by less than 0.1%.

What this check cannot test is the solder model itself. A one-element ANSYS run of the same Anand law, driven through my corner-bump history, is set up and is the next item.

What the model leaves out

  • The bumps are smeared into one layer. Real corner bumps also see peeling and bending at the joint, and Syed's constants were fitted to volume-averaged energy from detailed 3D models with a different creep law. I trust the absolute cycle counts to within a factor of about two, and the trends and ratios much more.
  • No lid, stiffener ring or BGA balls. These change warpage a great deal and add a second fatigue site, the substrate-to-board joints.
  • One stress-free temperature, linear elastic adherends, and no solder aging. SAC305 softens as it ages, which shortens life.
  • Underfill only buys 2.4× life here. The usual figure for flip chips on organic substrates is ten times or more. With a full area array under a stiff 0.9 mm silicon stack, even bare bumps carry enough force to couple the plates. That is the result I most want to check with a discrete-bump model.

What I took from it

  • Warpage and bump fatigue can move independently. Interposer size changes one, underfill modulus changes the other, and only substrate CTE improves both at once.
  • A cycle count means little without a distribution. The median is 2,600 cycles, but 1% of parts fail by about 1,200.
  • Thermal design and reliability are one problem. The junction temperature my cold plate sets is the field load here. Cutting the 31 K load swing by 10 K would raise the acceleration factor about three times.
