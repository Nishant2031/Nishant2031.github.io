Microchannel cold plate for a 1 kW AI accelerator

A design tool that finds the cold plate geometry holding a 1,000 W chip under its temperature limit for the least pumping power, benchmarked against a 1981 experiment and against a conjugate heat transfer solver I wrote from scratch.

The problem

AI accelerators now dissipate around a kilowatt from a die a few centimetres across, which is beyond what air cooling can remove. The industry answer is direct-to-chip liquid cooling: a copper cold plate with fine channels clamped onto the package.

I set a design target that looks like a current GPU or ASIC: 1,000 W from a 30 × 30 mm die (111 W/cm² on average, about 220 W/cm² in hotspots), cooled by a 25% propylene glycol mix entering at 35 °C, with the junction held below 85 °C.

Approach

  1. Thermal resistance network. Heat crosses the die, the thermal interface material, spreads in the copper base, enters the coolant through the fins, and warms the coolant. Each step is one resistance.
  2. Convection and friction. Shah and London laminar duct correlations with Stephan's entry-length correction, fin efficiency for the channel walls, and an apparent friction factor for developing flow.
  3. Design sweep. 720 combinations of channel width, fin width, channel height and flow rate, filtered to laminar flow and a fin aspect ratio a skiving process can make. 600 designs remain.
  4. Selection. Lowest pumping power with at least 12 K of junction margin for hotspots and under 50 kPa pressure drop.
  5. Hotspot map. A 2D finite-difference solve of the base with a non-uniform power map and coolant that warms along the flow.

Benchmark against published experiments

A model agreeing with my own solver only shows the two are consistent. The real test is measured data, so I ran the code against the experiment that started this field: Tuckerman and Pease's 1981 paper, which cooled 790 W/cm² from a 1 cm² silicon chip with 50 µm water channels. They published three heat sinks with measured pressure drop and thermal resistance.

I ran my correlations at their geometry and conditions, with silicon and water in place of copper and glycol. Nothing was tuned to their data.

Design #1 lands almost exactly. The other two do not, and the reasons are worth more than the agreement:

  • Constant fluid properties. My correlations evaluate viscosity at the bulk temperature. At 790 W/cm² the channel wall is near 94 °C while the bulk is near 34 °C, so the water next to the wall is less than half as viscous and the flow slips through more easily than a constant-property model allows. Re-running design #3 with the viscosity taken at the hottest wall temperature gives 159 kPa against the measured 214 kPa — the measurement sits between the two, which is where it should be, since the true mean wall temperature lies between bulk and peak.
  • An unknown I cannot resolve. The measured resistance includes conduction through the silicon between their heater and the channel roots, and that thickness is not given in the paper. It is worth about 7 mK/W per 100 µm, roughly 8% of the measured value, which is the error bar in the centre panel.
  • Design #2 does not reconcile. It carries 38% more flow than design #1 through a smaller cross-section, so any laminar model requires about 1.7 times the pressure drop; the published value rises by 1.13 times. No property correction closes a gap in that direction. I cannot explain it from the published numbers alone, and I would want the as-etched channel dimensions before trusting that point.

The practical conclusion for my own design: it runs at 111 W/cm² with roughly 2.5 K between wall and coolant, so the constant-property assumption that breaks down in their 790 W/cm² case is comfortably safe in mine. The benchmark tells me where my model stops being trustworthy, which is above roughly 200 W/cm².

Checking my own design numerically

The benchmark above tests the correlations against someone else’s hardware. To check the design I actually selected, I solved it a second way. I wrote a finite-volume conjugate heat transfer solver for one half-channel (copper fin and base plus coolant, 8.8 million cells) and a 3D conduction solve for heat spreading in the base, then compared.

Hydraulics agree to better than 1%. The two thermal errors are each about 11% and have opposite signs, so the total lands within 5% and on the conservative side. Refining the mesh by a factor of 39 moved the convection resistance by 0.9% and the pressure drop by 1.0%.

The check has limits of its own: it assumes fully developed velocity (the entry length is about 0.6 mm of the 60 mm channel), ignores heat conduction along the flow direction, and has no inlet or outlet manifolds.

What I took from it

  • Past about 200 µm channels, the cold plate is no longer the bottleneck. The remaining resistance is in the die, the interface material and the base, which is a packaging problem and the kind of interface physics I measure in my PhD work.
  • The coldest design is a poor design. Chasing the last 7 K costs 90 times the pumping power, multiplied across every server in a rack.
  • Flow direction should follow the floorplan. With a 15 K coolant rise, a hotspot placed downstream runs hotter than the same hotspot upstream.

Next steps

  • Run the same half-channel in ANSYS Fluent with full Navier–Stokes and manifolds, as a third independent result.
  • Model the plate in SolidWorks — manifolds, ports, O-ring groove — so the design is a manufacturable part rather than a set of dimensions.
  • Add temperature-dependent fluid properties, the limitation the benchmark exposed.
  • Replace the spreading formula in the design tool with the 3D solve, removing the largest single error.
  • Add thermo-mechanical stress in the interface layer under power cycling.
